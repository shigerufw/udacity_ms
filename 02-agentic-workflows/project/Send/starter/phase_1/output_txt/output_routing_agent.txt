Rome, the capital of Italy, has a rich and complex history that spans over two and a half millennia. It was founded, according to legend, in 753 BC by Romulus, who became its first king after killing his brother Remus. The early Roman Kingdom was characterized by a monarchical system, which lasted until around 509 BC when the last king, Tarquin the Proud, was overthrown, leading to the establishment of the Roman Republic.

During the Republic (509–27 BC), Rome expanded its territory through military conquests and alliances, becoming a dominant power in the Mediterranean. This period saw significant political developments, including the creation of a complex system of checks and balances, the Senate's power, and the struggle between the patricians (aristocratic families) and plebeians (common citizens).

The Republic faced internal strife, including class conflicts and civil wars, notably the conflict between Julius Caesar and Pompey. Caesar's rise to power culminated in his appointment as dictator for life in 44 BC, but his assassination that same year led to further civil wars. Ultimately, Caesar's adopted heir, Octavian (later known as Augustus), emerged victorious, marking the end of the Republic and the beginning of the Roman Empire in 27 BC.

The Roman Empire expanded to its greatest territorial extent under Emperor Trajan in the early 2nd century AD, encompassing vast regions across Europe, North Africa, and parts of the Middle East. This period is often referred to as the Pax Romana, a time of relative peace and stability.

However, the Empire faced numerous challenges, including economic troubles, military defeats, and internal divisions. By the late 3rd century AD, the Empire was split into the Western Roman Empire and the Eastern Roman Empire (Byzantine Empire) for administrative efficiency. The Western Roman Empire, with Rome as its capital, faced increasing pressure from barbarian invasions and ultimately fell in 476 AD when the last emperor, Romulus Augustulus, was deposed.

Rome continued to be an important center during the Middle Ages, becoming the seat of the Papacy and a focal point for Christianity. The Renaissance, beginning in the 14th century, saw a revival of art, culture, and learning in Rome, leading to significant architectural and artistic achievements.

In the 19th century, Rome became the capital of the newly unified Kingdom of Italy in 1871. The city underwent modernization and expansion, and it has continued to grow and evolve into the vibrant capital it is today, known for its historical landmarks, cultural heritage, and political significance within Italy and Europe.
